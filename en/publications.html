International Review of Economics & Finance · Volume 111 · October 2026 · Article 105735

Doing well by doing good? Quasi-experimental evidence from corporate philanthropy in banking

Marcelo J. Villena, Mauricio G. Villena and Franco Antonucci

Abstract. This paper asks whether a highly visible corporate-philanthropy campaign raises a bank’s market valuation. We study Bank of Chile’s two-decade role as official sponsor of the national Telethon, using daily share prices for all four listed Chilean banks between 2000 and 2020, spanning sixteen editions of the campaign. We compare Bank of Chile’s stock performance around each campaign with that of the other banks, first using share-price levels and then using measures that strip out ordinary market movements, such as daily returns and abnormal returns. Price levels suggest a positive, significant effect, seemingly supporting the idea that doing good pays off financially. This turns out to be an artifact of how stock prices drift over time: once we look at returns instead of levels, the effect disappears entirely, becoming negligible and statistically indistinguishable from zero, consistently across benchmarks, estimation choices, and each edition. We conclude that this sponsorship left no measurable footprint on shareholder value, neither a benefit nor a cost. If such philanthropy creates value, it likely accrues gradually, through customer loyalty and reputation, rather than through daily stock prices. The broader implication is methodological: claims about the financial effects of one-off corporate events should be tested on outcomes that filter out ordinary price trends, not raw price levels, which can manufacture an effect that is not really there. A limitation is that we measure sponsorship visibility rather than the undisclosed monetary value of donations, and that only four banks are listed on the exchange, limiting statistical power.

Applied Economics Letters · Published online: 13 Aug 2026

Does external vulnerability predict sovereign rating-at-risk?

Mauricio G. Villena and María J. Quinteros

Abstract. Sovereign ratings are persistent, so rating-level regressions may miss changes in downside credit risk. We propose rating-at-risk: the probability and magnitude of future rating deterioration conditional on the inherited rating. In a panel of 68 emerging and developing economies, external vulnerability predicts 1- and 2-year downgrade risk and multi-year notch losses, with the signal concentrated in the upper tail of the vulnerability distribution. Macroeconomic controls attenuate pooled binary estimates but leave the within-country and cumulative notch-loss evidence intact. External vulnerability improves country-blocked validation modestly but not temporal validation over 2018–2023, so we interpret rating-at-risk as a transition-risk summary rather than a forecasting rule.

DOI: 10.1080/13504851.2026.2717404
